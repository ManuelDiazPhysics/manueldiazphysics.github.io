Black hole thermodynamics with membranes

With David Kastor

When the cosmological constant is permitted to vary, the mass of a black hole corresponds to its enthalpy. Membranes that alter gravitational couplings across their surfaces transform this variation into a physical process.

I investigate whether charged membranes that modify Newton's constant and the gravitational couplings of higher curvature terms in Lovelock gravity can carry negative energy outside a black hole, specifically in membrane ergospheres, and whether such energy can be absorbed in a manner consistent with black hole thermodynamics. Lovelock gravity generalizes general relativity to higher dimensions while preserving the second order nature of the field equations. A similar question arises for Born-Infeld black holes.

Quantum black holes as fluctuating geometries

With Martin Bojowald

In canonical effective methods, a quantum state is described by the expectation values of the canonical operators and by their fluctuations and correlations. With Bojowald, I used these methods to show that semiclassical quantum corrections can leave the constraint algebra of spherically symmetric gravity unchanged, which made it possible to study static quantum black holes. For generic semiclassical states, these black holes and their superpositions are fluctuating geometries, whose quantum corrections to Newton's potential are first order in the Planck length, larger than the second order ones of perturbative quantum gravity.

In the asymptotic approximation of this model, far from the black hole, I showed that requiring the corrected line element of a static quantum black hole to be covariant bounds fluctuations of a metric component from above, which the uncertainty principle alone cannot do. Together with the uncertainty principle, this bound also limits fluctuations of its momentum, the extrinsic curvature of spatial slices, from below, up to an uncertainty product of the state, which must be fixed to make predictions. In this approximation, the upper bound means that the area variance of a sphere far from the black hole is at most of order its area in Planck units. Zurek and collaborators have derived a lower bound of the same order for a causal diamond (Ciambelli, He and Zurek, 2025). Together, the two bounds restrict which quantum states are allowed and set a minimum size for the quantum correction to Newton's potential, which the figure above shows. A black hole that is static on average is then a superposition of collapsing and expanding geometries. This effect is negligible for black holes much larger than the Planck length but could matter for primordial black holes.

The fluctuations and correlations considered here correspond to the second order moments of the state. The moments page details which aspects are retained and which are omitted in a description based on moments.

Next, I want to know what these fluctuations look like at the horizon, once nonspherical perturbations and correlations between points are included, and whether quantum oscillations of perturbed superpositions emit gravitational waves.

Quantum simulation of constrained systems

The quantities that a quantum simulation will estimate from a sequence of measurements are precisely the expectation values, the fluctuations and the correlations that are the very quantities that canonical effective methods are designed to compute. My aim is to use those methods to predict the outcome of the simulations of constrained systems, to diagnose the sorts of errors that break the constraints and to find out where simple truncations are insufficient. The bounce on the quantum register is the obvious first benchmark case which can be solved exactly.

Two cutoffs

In the qubit calculation I retain the lowest 2n volume eigenstates (n being the number of qubits). In the moment equations I retain all moments up to order N. For the solvable bounce I can vary n and N independently and compare the two calculations against the exact evolution. Once an interaction is turned on I have to check convergence in n before using the qubit results to judge the moment expansion. What value of N do I need in order to follow the bounce to a given accuracy?

A bounce on a quantum register

In the solvable model of a universe full of a free massless scalar field the volume bounces rather than collapses and the equations close exactly. With the scalar φ as a clock the average volume is Vb cosh φ and the fluctuations have exact equations as well.

A quantum computer with n qubits can represent the lowest 2n volume levels and will only track the exact bounce for a finite period of time. Each additional qubit gives you roughly ln 2 units of relational time.

The dotted curve satisfies the second order moment equations. In this model the mean volume, the mean rate of change of volume with respect to φ, the two variances of those quantities and their covariance all satisfy closed linear equations and so the curve is consistent with the exact solution.

The register as a lattice of qubits and the volume levels it contains from the lowest at the bottom to the top level. The gold bars indicate the state at the bounce. Each additional qubit doubles the number of levels so the state has further to grow before it gets to the top level (the dashed line on the chart) and the register will follow the exact bounce for a longer period of time.

This scenario was simulated classically, using the model from M. Bojowald, Phys. Rev. D 75, 081301 (2007). The Gaussian state has Vb = 8.5 volume units at the bounce; the register is considered to deviate from the exact result when the difference exceeds one part in a thousand.

I used Qiskit to simulate a quantum circuit representing a universe that contracts, reaches a minimum volume, and then expands. Since an exact solution exists, I was able to determine for how long the circuit reproduced the mean volume.

With a large time step, both the 6 and 8 qubit circuits lost accuracy at approximately the same point. Smaller time steps enabled the 8 qubit circuit to track the exact solution for a longer duration, albeit at the expense of a significantly increased number of gates. This leads to the following question: can a circuit employing the model's structure achieve the same level of accuracy with fewer gates as the number of qubits increases?

Moments of a tunneling state

My papers with Martin Bojowald and collaborators on quantum black holes and spacetime superpositions describe the quantum state through its low order moments, and these approximations do not extend to the horizon. This raises the question of how many moments are required to describe a state. The register above addresses this question in terms of qubits; here it is addressed in terms of moments, using a particle tunneling out of a well, whose exact evolution can be computed and whose state quickly ceases to be Gaussian.

A particle initially at rest in a well behind a barrier is described by the Gaussian ground state of the harmonic approximation to the well, representing a state of minimum uncertainty permitted by the uncertainty principle, Δq Δp = ħ/2. The Schrödinger equation is solved exactly, and the state exhibits tunneling through the barrier.

The exact state is approximated statically at each moment in time using its position moments up to order N, from which the curve of maximum entropy is drawn. At certain times the exact state requires a different number of moments for a better approximation. Initially, the state is Gaussian, and the second order is exact. Once the state splits across the barrier, the second order is unable to represent the portion that has passed through it, while the fourth order is able to do so. By the end, even the eighth order fails to capture part of the shape.

In the lower plot is the probability of finding the particle past the top of the barrier. It is much closer to the exact one than the shape of the state is, in part because of the way the curves are drawn (maximum entropy). The moments by themselves can only bound it: with the first four moments at t = 4 it can be anywhere from 0.15 to 0.68 for distributions on the range of q shown, and the exact value is 0.38 while the maximum entropy one is 0.36.

Top: The filled region represents the probability density of the exact state at time t, while the dashed line depicts the distribution reconstructed from position moments up to the specified order. The dotted line indicates the top of the barrier, and the light curve represents the potential. Bottom: The probability of locating the particle beyond the top of the barrier is plotted as a function of time for both the exact solution and the specified order. Dots indicate the current time; Play animates the curves up to this point.

For order N, the curve represents the maximum entropy distribution among all distributions sharing the moments ⟨q⟩, ⟨q2⟩, …, ⟨qN⟩ of the exact state, without additional assumptions. Only position moments are considered; canonical effective approaches utilize both position and momentum moments, which are related by the uncertainty principle. The state does not evolve according to the moment equations. The exact state is characterized by a truncated set of moments. The potential is given by V(q) = (27/4) Vtop γ q²(q − 1)(q − 1/γ), with Vtop = 1, γ = 1/2, ħ = 1/4, and unit mass. The top of the barrier is located at q ≈ 0.61, where V ≈ 0.68. Vtop equals the barrier height only in the limit γ → 0.

Black holes and inflation

With Jennie Traschen and Sofia Corbà

I'm looking at how a black hole left over from a preinflationary era could affect the amount of inflation associated with a given potential. In the standard case the inflaton is constant on slices of constant cosmic time and the number of e-foldings measures how much a region grows between two such slices. In the presence of a black hole the inflaton is not constant on these slices. Gregory, Kastor and Traschen found slow roll solutions in which the inflaton is a function only of a radially dependent time coordinate T (2017, 2018). In the paper we're currently writing with Sofia Corbà and Jennie Traschen we use this framework to count e-foldings by comparing the spatial volumes of the final and initial inflating regions on slices of constant T which gives the standard count in the absence of a black hole.

The next step is to complete the incorporation of the back reaction of the evolving inflaton on the metric.

Covariant cosmology and the bounce

With Martin Bojowald and Erick Duque

In GR the bracket of two Hamiltonian constraints has the inverse spatial metric as its structure function. If a modified constraint changes that structure function then it defines an emergent spatial metric. Covariance then requires an additional condition on top of the closure of the constraint algebra. That's the principle of emergent modified gravity. With Bojowald and Erick Duque I extended these conditions to perturbed cosmological backgrounds and found that the known loop quantum cosmology models either aren't covariant or still have a physical singularity despite their bounce.

My current research is focused on building bounces which are both covariant and completely free of singularities, with no new degrees of freedom introduced, so that perturbations can simply evolve through them. The primordial spectrum which results from such a bounce is a key prediction. At PHENO 2026 I presented emergent modified gravity as a model of dynamical dark energy with no new degrees of freedom, motivated by the preference for evolving dark energy in the DESI data.

Before the bounce

A dark sector phase transition

I looked at the BM1 benchmark of Ghosh, Guo, Han and Liu (dark SU(2) sector coupled to the SM via the Higgs portal). In my leading order high T calculation the dark field w3 develops a nonzero value continuously at 191.5 GeV, and a subsequent first order transition nucleates at 163.5 GeV and percolates at 159.1 GeV (for a bubble wall speed of 0.9c). The nucleation temperature is about 92% of the critical temperature (177.1 GeV) so the supercooling is quite mild. The transition strength α (defined from the latent heat) is 0.115 at nucleation and 0.154 at percolation, with β/H at 717 and 311 respectively. I've computed the tunneling actions with my own multifield solver (which uses the tunneling potential method of Espinosa and Konstandin (2019)).

Supernova progenitors

With J. Craig Wheeler

The Betelgeuse Project set out to answer one question, how we can know when Betelgeuse will explode. To find out, I produced simulations of supernova progenitors with the stellar evolution code MESA, and we compared them with the star's observed luminosity, temperature and radius. Its spin was the surprise. A star should slow down as it swells into a red supergiant, the way a figure skater slows down when she opens her arms, due to conservation of angular momentum. Betelgeuse appears to spin far faster than our single star models could account for, so its extra angular momentum must have come from somewhere, and Wheeler proposed that it came from a companion the star swallowed as it expanded. The simulations could follow the star only to the onset of core collapse. Wanting to know what happens after that, when gravity takes over, led me to my interest in black holes.
